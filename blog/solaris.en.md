Recently, I read **Solaris** by **Stanisław Lem** for the first time.

And I have to admit something that even surprises me – it’s one of those books I simply couldn’t put down, and I finished it in just two sittings.

I like reading. I’ve always liked it. As a child, I devoured adventure novels. Later, I increasingly reached for biographies and lifestyle guides. However, science fiction was never my natural choice. I tended to see it as a genre built around technology, spaceships, and distant visions of the future that didn’t quite move me. They simply felt too remote.

And yet *Solaris* completely changed my attitude.

### What is Solaris really about?

On the surface, it is the story of a psychologist who arrives at a research station orbiting a planet covered by a single, vast ocean. This ocean is not an ordinary geological phenomenon – it is a sentient being.

But *Solaris* is not a book about space. It is not even a book about an alien planet. It is a book about cognition. About the limits of human reason. About whether we are capable of understanding something that radically exceeds our experience.

Lem did not merely invent an abstract concept of an intelligent ocean. He created an entire history of its study: dozens of theories, scientific disputes, concepts, hypotheses, and interpretative schools. While reading, you feel as if you are encountering a real scientific discipline – “Solaristics” – complete with its publications, polemics, and dead ends.

That is what makes it extraordinary. The consistency. The intellectual precision. The fact that human nature does not change, even as technology advances.

### Is contact even possible?

One of the most striking ideas for me concerns the nature of contact with an alien civilization. The author poses a question: what if, even if we discover extraterrestrial life, it will not be humanoid, will not possess language, intentions, or logic that we are capable of understanding?

What if it surpasses us – intellectually, ontologically – in ways we cannot even imagine?

Or perhaps contact would lead nowhere at all. Perhaps both sides would be like two operating systems without a shared communication protocol.

This leads to another question: why are we searching in the first place?

Even today, we continue to search for life beyond Earth through programs such as SETI and space missions conducted by NASA and ESA. We look for biosignatures on exoplanets, traces of water on Mars, radio signals from deep space.

Officially, it is about knowledge. About understanding humanity’s place in the universe. About advancing science and technology.

But *Solaris* forces us to ask: do we truly want contact? Are we ready for it?

### The most devastating reflection

What struck me most, however, was another thought. If humans cannot understand one another – culturally, politically, emotionally – how could we possibly understand something radically different?

The ocean in *Solaris* is not hostile. It is simply different. And yet that difference proves unbearable. So unbearable that one of the characters takes his own life as a result of the ocean’s attempt at contact.

Perhaps the problem is not a lack of intelligence on the other side. Perhaps the problem is our expectation that whatever we encounter will be a reflection of ourselves. But why should it be? Evolution does not follow a single path, as we can see from the diversity of species on Earth. And even more so on another planet, where entirely different conditions may prevail. Or, as on Solaris, conditions that change over centuries because the planet orbits two stars.

### Why am I writing about this?

It has been a long time since I have read with such tension. With that feeling that I cannot set the book aside. That each new page will bring even more uncertainty.

Anyone who loves reading knows this experience. That state of focus in which the outside world ceases to exist.

This book definitively broke my impasse of finding books that are interesting but not truly absorbing.

But it is not only about a compelling plot. This book leaves me with something more unsettling. The thought that perhaps all of our knowledge is projection. That even when we look into space, we are really looking into a mirror. And that the greatest challenge is not understanding an alien ocean, but accepting that some things may remain beyond our reach forever.
